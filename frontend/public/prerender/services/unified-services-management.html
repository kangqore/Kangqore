Service management stopped being an IT discipline some time ago. Your employees raise a laptop request, an onboarding, a contract review, a facilities ticket and an expense query — and meet five different intake forms, five different response promises and five teams that cannot see each other’s queues.

Four Experiences, One Operating Model Underneath.

Total experience is usually run as a survey program. It is not one. Innovation, user, customer and business experience are four readings of the same thing — whether a request reaches an owner and gets answered. The operating model decides all four, which is why they move together or not at all.

Innovation Experience

Whether someone inside the business can get a new service stood up without it becoming a project. Most models route a request to try something into the same queue as a password reset, where it waits behind volume. The question is which requests are allowed a different path, and who is permitted to approve one.

  • Requests needing a new service, and how long they wait
  • Approval hops between an idea and a working service
  • Share of new services standing up outside the catalog
  • Time from request to a usable first version

User Experience

What it is actually like to raise a request here — how many places you have to know about, how often you re-explain the same thing, and whether anything tells you where it got to. Onboarding is the clearest test: a joiner touches IT, HR, facilities and access on day one, and meets your operating model rather than your intranet.

  • Intake routes an employee is expected to know
  • Requests where the same information is asked twice
  • Time a request sits with no owner attached
  • Day-one joiner completion across functions, not just IT

Customer Experience

The link most decks assert and few instrument. When an internal team cannot get an access request or a contract review cleared, the delay lands on a customer with the internal steps invisible to them. Working out which internal queues sit underneath customer-facing work is a service management question before it is a CX one.

  • Internal queues with customer-facing work behind them
  • Breaches on internal steps inside a customer commitment
  • Escalations traced back to an unowned internal request
  • Handoffs between functions on a single customer outcome

Business Experience

What the enterprise gets once the other three hold: capacity released from moving work around, a cost per request you can defend in a budget conversation, and enough evidence about where work backs up to make an operating decision. It is the layer executives are asked about, and the one that cannot be bought on its own.

  • Cost per request, by function and by channel
  • Effort spent moving work rather than doing it
  • Where volume concentrates, and whether it is avoidable
  • Commitments met without heroics or manual escalation

The platforms, and when to keep the one you have.

Platform choice is mostly settled by what you already license and by how far beyond IT you intend to go. These are the defaults and what overrides them.

  • Enterprise service management suites: The most capable option and the most expensive to own. Earns its cost when service management genuinely extends across functions; oversized for an IT-only desk, where the licensing outruns the benefit within about two years. Managed: ServiceNow. Self-hosted: Where the ambition is genuinely enterprise-wide.
  • Mid-market and IT-first platforms: Substantially cheaper and sufficient for most IT service desks. The constraint appears when you extend beyond IT: HR and legal workflows are possible but the model is less opinionated, so governance has to come from you. Managed: Jira Service Management, Freshservice, Ivanti. Self-hosted: Jira SM where engineering already lives there.
  • What you already license: The most common right answer and the least often proposed. Unused service management capability inside an existing estate is widespread, and we have no license margin to lose by pointing at it. Managed: Microsoft, SAP and Oracle estates often include workflow. Self-hosted: Frequently unconfigured rather than absent.
  • Experience and measurement: Operational metrics say the ticket closed on time. Experience measures say whether the person got what they needed. Both are required, and a deflection rate improved by making a human harder to reach is not an improvement. Managed: XLA tooling, sentiment capture, survey platforms. Self-hosted: Instrumented in the workflow, not bolted on.
  • Integration and identity: Ownership is only real if the platform knows who someone is, what they own and who approves for them. That data comes from elsewhere, and the integration is usually the critical path rather than the configuration. Managed: Identity providers, HR systems, asset and finance platforms. Self-hosted: Engineered on our integration service.
  • Automation and virtual agents: Automating a request type nobody agreed the definition of encodes the disagreement. Self-service deflects well when the catalog is real and badly when it is a list written for an audit. Managed: Platform-native automation, conversational interfaces. Self-hosted: After the taxonomy, never before it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is unified service management, and how is it different from ITSM?

ITSM is service management applied to IT. Unified service management is the same discipline applied across every function that receives requests from employees — IT, HR, facilities, legal, finance, procurement — on one taxonomy, one ownership model and one definition of what a response commitment means.

The word doing the work is unified, and it is about the operating model rather than the tool. Five functions configuring five corners of one platform is a single license and five operating models. It looks unified on a procurement schedule and does not feel unified to anybody raising a request.

What makes it one service is upstream of any product: an agreed service taxonomy, one intake, one clock standard, and ownership that is never null.

We already bought ServiceNow. What would you do that we have not?

Probably nothing to the platform, and quite a lot to what it is configured against.

The common pattern is a competent IT rollout followed by each other function being given a corner to configure. Two years later HR has its own request types, facilities has its own priority scale, legal defines a working day differently, and the cross-function report has to be assembled by hand because no two definitions agree.

That is not a platform problem and no amount of further configuration fixes it. The work is the taxonomy, the ownership model and the commitment definitions, agreed across functions and then imposed on the configuration rather than derived from it.

Where the answer genuinely is platform work, our ServiceNow practice does that build. This service decides what should be built and why.

How far beyond IT should we actually go?

Function by function, and slower than the business case assumes.

HR is usually second because the volume is high and the request types are well understood. Facilities follows because the workflows are simple and the win is visible. Legal and finance are harder — confidentiality constraints, approval hierarchies that mirror delegated authority, and request types that are genuinely bespoke.

The failure mode is rolling three functions simultaneously to hit a program milestone. Each one negotiates its own exceptions under time pressure, and you arrive at one platform carrying five operating models, which is exactly the state you were trying to leave.

One function, measured against the baseline, before the next one starts. It is slower on the plan and faster in practice.

Our service catalog has four hundred entries and nobody uses it. Why?

Because it was written to be complete rather than to be requested, and completeness is an audit virtue rather than a usability one.

Measure it. Pull the request count per catalog entry for the last four quarters and you will typically find a small fraction of entries carrying almost all the volume, a long tail requested once or twice, and a meaningful number never requested at all. The tail is not harmless — it is what makes the catalog unsearchable, which is why people email instead.

The fix is unpopular and simple: prune on measured usage, write entries in the words employees use rather than the words the owning team uses, and re-measure quarterly. A catalog is a product with a usage metric, not a document with a sign-off.

What is an XLA, and is it worth bothering with?

An experience-level agreement measures whether the person got what they needed, alongside the service level that measures whether the ticket closed on time. The two disagree more often than most service owners expect.

It is worth bothering with, and it is easy to do badly. An XLA built from a satisfaction survey attached to ticket closure measures the people who replied, which is not the people who gave up. Better signals are the ones already in your data: reopened requests, requests raised twice for the same thing, time spent unowned, and how often somebody bypassed the process entirely.

We would rather instrument three of those honestly than run a survey that peaks after a relaunch and tells you nothing by month six.

How do you handle multiple outsourced suppliers?

With a service integration model, because the alternative is a retained team spending its week deciding whose fault something is.

SIAM means one accountable integration layer sitting above suppliers, owning the end-to-end commitment, the shared toolchain and the data that lets you compare them. The hard parts are contractual rather than technical: aligned definitions across contracts written at different times, agreed data exchange, and a major incident process that works when three suppliers are in the bridge.

The common failure is buying the tooling and skipping the contract alignment. Then every cross-supplier incident becomes a commercial negotiation conducted while the service is down.

Should we automate first or fix the process first?

Process first, and this is one of the few places in technology where the sequencing is not arguable.

Automating a request type nobody agreed the definition of encodes the disagreement and makes it harder to change. Self-service built on a catalog written for an audit deflects badly, and the deflection rate improves mainly because reaching a human got harder — which reads as success in the report and as failure to everyone using it.

Once the taxonomy is real and the ownership model holds, automation is genuinely effective: the highest-volume request types are usually simple, well understood and worth removing from human hands entirely.

Who owns the model after you leave?

A named service owner inside your organization, and if that role does not exist the engagement has failed regardless of what was configured.

A service owner needs the authority to refuse an exception. Most unified models decay because a function asks for a variation under deadline pressure, gets it, and the taxonomy quietly forks. Somebody has to be able to say no, and be backed when they do.

On the delivery side: configuration documented and version-controlled, workflows a business analyst can read, runbooks covering the failure modes we actually hit, and your people trained during the work rather than in a week at the end. Managed operations exists and is priced separately. What we will not build is a model only we can run.

How do you measure whether it worked?

Against four numbers captured before anything changes, and reported afterwards whether or not they flatter us.

How many requests arrive through the governed intake rather than through email, chat or a colleague. How much time requests spend with no named owner. How many times a request changes hands before reaching the team that resolves it. And how much of the published catalog anybody actually requested this quarter.

All four are countable from your own tooling this week, which is the point — a number you can check reads differently from one you cannot. Deliberately absent: deflection rate, which improves when a human becomes harder to reach, and satisfaction scores, which peak after any relaunch and say nothing by month six.
