Internet of Things, Engineered End to End.

From homes to industries to enterprises, connected devices have become commonplace, and getting them connected is no longer the hard part. What is hard is that a connected product is four products at once. There is a board, firmware on it, a platform it reports to, and an application somebody uses, and every one of them constrains the other three.

That is why these programs slip late rather than early. A power budget assumed in month one, a protocol chosen for convenience, a certification region nobody listed, an asset identity that differs between two systems. None of it looks like a problem at the time. All of it surfaces in month nine, when the answer is a respin and a respin is weeks of fabrication before anybody can even confirm the fix.

So we make the expensive decisions first and in one place. Hardware, platform and application are shaped together rather than handed between teams. Pre-compliance runs before design freeze. The contract between firmware and cloud is written before either side is. The asset model is settled before integration starts.

The result is not a faster build. It is a build without the quarter that usually disappears between the prototype that worked and the fleet you are allowed to ship.

Consulting and Solution Development

Deciding what to build and why, and whether to build it at all, before anything is committed to hardware.

  • Requirement elicitation and analysis: Establish what the deployment has to do, and the connectivity, physical environment, service life and certification region it has to do it in. Each of those eliminates options before any preference applies, and they are what requirement documents usually omit.
  • Business process modeling with technology and process consulting: Model the process the devices are meant to serve, since instrumenting a plant or a field operation nobody has examined produces telemetry about a problem rather than a solution to it.
  • Defining the problem statement along with recommendation on solution blueprint: A written problem statement and a blueprint you can challenge. The decisions it settles, protocol, silicon, form factor, are the ones that get fabricated, and a fabricated decision is a respin rather than an edit.
  • Thought leadership and repository of business-ready use cases: Reference architectures for connected products that have been built before, covering the parts that are always the same, device identity, update path and the boundary to your existing systems.
  • Product and service enhancements: What the deployment should become once units are live, which is constrained by what the fleet already in the field can actually be updated to rather than by what the roadmap would prefer.

Intelligent Platforms

The platform your fleet reports to, built to accept devices and vendors you have not chosen yet.

  • Open platform architecture with end-to-end connectivity: Device to edge to cloud to application as one path, so a message has a defined route rather than an accumulation of integrations.
  • Multi-tenant architecture to support IoT resources cost efficiently and securely: Tenancy decided at design, since retrofitting isolation once fleets share a platform is the expensive kind of rework.
  • Big data support with mashup builder and business intelligence: Telemetry at fleet volume made queryable by people who did not build it, because data only one team can reach is a cost rather than an asset.
  • Device vendor agnostic and device virtualization: An abstraction over the hardware, so changing a sensor supplier is a procurement decision rather than a platform migration.
  • Secure, reliable, scalable and OTA firmware updates: Signed updates with staged rollout and enforced rollback, because a fleet you cannot recover is a fleet you cannot safely update.

Connected Device

The hardware itself, from schematic and layout through fabrication to regulatory certification.

  • Hardware product design (PCB, firmware) to deliver end-to-end solutions and accelerate time to market: Board and firmware developed together, since the two constrain each other and finding that out late means a respin.
  • Design flow implementation and PCB fabrication support: The path from layout to a manufacturable board, with component availability treated as a design constraint rather than a purchasing surprise.
  • Device IoT framework and enablement: Connectivity, identity and update handled by a framework rather than reimplemented per product, so your second device costs less than your first.
  • Customized form-factor design and prototyping as per requirement: Enclosure and form factor built to where the device will actually live, including the environments nobody photographs.

Testing

Verification on real devices in the conditions they will meet, not on an emulator that forgives them.

  • Device lab testing (manual and automated): Functional, interoperability and stress testing on production hardware under controlled conditions.
  • Device field testing: Behavior under the interference, temperature and connectivity the device will actually meet, which is where bench results stop applying.
  • Platform testing: Ingestion, storage and processing exercised at fleet scale rather than at demonstration scale.
  • Mobile application testing: Real handset coverage across the versions your users hold, not the ones the team develops on.
  • Application automated testing: Regression running against every change, so a suite cannot quietly drift behind the code it tests.

What the deployment has to satisfy, sector by sector.

The engineering is much the same across these sectors. What changes is who certifies your device, what a failure costs you, and which obligations decide the design before any preference does.

Manufacturing & Industrial

A device that reaches the plant floor carries consequences an office system does not.

  • Separation between operational technology and corporate networks preserved
  • Industrial protocols supported natively rather than through a fragile bridge
  • Maintenance windows aligned to production schedules rather than to ours
  • Condition monitoring and predictive maintenance built on your own failure data
  • Long support horizons for equipment that outlives its vendor
  • Safety relevant behavior verified independently of the build team

Healthcare & Life Sciences

A connected device that reaches a patient is certified before it ships, not after.

  • Regulatory classification settled before architecture rather than after
  • Design history and traceability maintained as the device is built
  • Cybersecurity expectations for connected clinical devices designed in
  • Patient data classified, encrypted and retained to the required standard
  • Post market surveillance and field update paths planned from the start
  • Usability engineering evidenced against the applicable standard

Energy & Utilities

The estate is remote, long lived and frequently beyond your own network.

  • Low power wide area connectivity where devices must last years on a battery
  • Physical security assumed, because somebody will open one of your devices
  • Certificate rotation planned, since an expired fleet certificate is a total outage
  • Data residency and critical infrastructure obligations settled at design
  • Intermittent connectivity treated as normal rather than as an error condition
  • Replacement windows planned for devices that cannot be updated

Retail & eCommerce

Devices sit in stores nobody on your team visits.

  • Installation designed for staff who are not engineers
  • Zero touch provisioning, because manual onboarding does not scale to estates
  • Cardholder data scope kept away from the device by design
  • Peak trading periods frozen deliberately rather than hopefully
  • Store connectivity constraints reflected in the architecture
  • Remote diagnosis before anyone is sent to a site

Media & Entertainment

The product runs on screens and devices nobody in the building owns.

  • Connected television and set top coverage on real hardware
  • Playback verified across network conditions rather than on the office link
  • Firmware fragmentation handled across devices nobody has updated in years
  • Audience and usage data modeled for households rather than single viewers
  • Content rights and territory constraints respected by the architecture
  • Accessibility built into the device experience rather than audited later

Frequently Asked Questions

What should an IoT program actually deliver?

Six things, and a program that cannot say which of them it exists for usually delivers none of them.

Connect and scale with efficiency. Analyze and act on new data. Integrate and transform the business processes the devices serve. Improve decision making with augmented intelligence rather than with more dashboards. Design, develop, integrate, deploy and manage the whole process end to end. And integrate the result with the enterprise architecture you already run rather than beside it.

That span is deliberate, from integrating the right sensors and deriving the insight through to choosing the platform that fits. The value is rarely in any one step, which is why programs that buy the steps separately spend their time at the seams between them.

Getting there means understanding where value is actually created and then systematically addressing the challenges underneath it, which is the unglamorous half of the work. Done properly the result is business value you can point at, better efficiency, and a lower operations and maintenance cost across the fleet lifetime.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

We have an idea for a connected product. What happens first?

We write down the problem the devices are meant to solve, and the constraints that will decide the architecture, because in connected products those constraints are unusually unforgiving.

Power budget, connectivity, physical environment, certification region and expected service life each eliminate options, and they eliminate them before any preference applies. A device that must run five years on a battery is a different product from one with mains power, and that is an architecture decision rather than a feature.

So the first stage produces a problem statement and a blueprint you can argue with. It usually names fewer options than people expect, because the constraints have already removed most of them.

What it avoids is the common failure: choosing a platform first and discovering in month nine that the hardware it assumed cannot meet the power budget.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

Should we build custom hardware or use something off the shelf?

Off the shelf unless you can name what it cannot do, because custom hardware carries a cost most business cases underestimate by an order of magnitude.

A custom board means design, prototype, certification, a supply chain, and a support horizon measured in years. Each respin is weeks of fabrication before anyone can confirm the fix. Against that, an existing module that is ninety per cent right is usually the better trade.

Custom earns its place where form factor, power, cost at volume or a function genuinely unavailable off the shelf decides it. At volume the unit economics can flip decisively, and that is a real reason rather than a preference.

We would rather run that comparison properly than assume either answer. It is a few weeks of work and it changes the program.

The question that settles it fastest is volume. Below a few thousand units the engineering rarely amortizes; well above that, the per unit saving usually pays for the whole design effort inside the first production run.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

How do we handle devices already in the field that we cannot update?

Carefully, and the first honest step is to establish exactly what is out there, which is usually less clear than expected.

Every deployed estate has devices at firmware levels nobody planned, units that stopped reporting years ago, and a few nobody can locate. An inventory built from what the fleet actually reports rather than from what was shipped is where this starts.

For devices that genuinely cannot be updated, the answer is containment: network segmentation, a gateway that mediates what they can reach, and a planned replacement window rather than an indefinite exception.

What we would not do is design the new platform around the constraints of devices you intend to retire. That is how a temporary compatibility layer becomes permanent architecture.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

How do you keep an IoT deployment secure?

By giving every device its own identity and never trusting the network, then making sure you can update them when something is found.

The characteristic failures are unglamorous: shared credentials across a fleet, an unauthenticated local interface, keys in firmware anyone can extract from a board. Each is a design decision that was convenient at prototype and expensive after deployment.

Per device identity, signed firmware, encrypted transport and a working update path cover most of it. The update path matters most, because it is the only control that still helps after a vulnerability is published.

Physical access has to be assumed. Somebody will take one of your devices apart, and the design should survive it.

The other assumption worth making is that a vulnerability will be published in a component you did not write. What matters then is not whether you were affected but how quickly you can tell, and how quickly you can ship a fix to units already in the field.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

Cellular, wifi, LoRa or something else?

Decided by power budget, message size, deployment density and who controls the site, and those four answers usually make the choice for you.

A device sending a few bytes hourly on a battery for years is a low power wide area problem. A camera is not. A device in a customer building you do not control cannot assume wifi credentials will ever be entered.

The cost that surprises people is not the radio, it is the connectivity contract across the fleet lifetime, and the certification work per region. Both belong in the business case from the start.

Hybrid is common and it is fine, as long as the platform treats the transport as an implementation detail rather than building a second architecture around it.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

How much data should we send, and how much should stay at the edge?

Send what someone will act on, process at the edge whatever the latency, bandwidth or connectivity makes impractical to send.

The common mistake is sending everything because storage is cheap. Storage is cheap; transport across a cellular fleet is not, and neither is querying telemetry nobody defined a use for. Estates accumulate years of data nobody has ever read.

Edge processing earns its place for control loops that cannot tolerate a round trip, for bandwidth that would be uneconomic, and for sites where connectivity is genuinely intermittent.

We would decide it per signal rather than as a platform policy, and revisit it once the deployment shows which data was actually used.

What does certification actually involve?

More than most plans allow, and it is the part that cannot be compressed by adding people.

A connected product typically needs radio compliance per region, electrical safety, and often an industry scheme on top. Each has a queue at an accredited laboratory, and a failure means a change, a respin and a new slot in that queue.

So pre compliance scanning happens while the layout can still change. Finding an emissions problem before design freeze is an afternoon with an engineer; finding it after is a quarter.

And the region list belongs in the plan at the start. Adding a market later is a fresh submission, not an amendment.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

How do you test something that lives in a factory or a field?

On real hardware in the lab, then in the conditions the device will actually meet, because the second regularly contradicts the first.

Lab testing covers function, interoperability and stress under controlled conditions and it is necessary. Field testing covers interference, temperature, vibration, and the connectivity that drops for reasons nobody can reproduce indoors.

Automation makes this sustainable. Field device test frameworks exercise real units continuously, and simulators for the peer protocols mean a build can be tested without assembling the entire physical estate every time.

What does not work is treating a successful bench demonstration as evidence. It tests the bench.

The useful discipline is to build the test estate as early as the product, because a device that cannot be tested automatically will be tested manually, and manual testing quietly sets the ceiling on how often you can release.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

How do we connect this to the systems we already run?

Through published event contracts rather than point to point integrations, because the second approach is cheaper once and more expensive every time after.

The value of a connected product is usually realized inside systems that already exist. A reading that does not reach the maintenance plan, the customer record or the invoice is telemetry rather than a business outcome.

What makes this hard is rarely the protocol. It is that asset identity differs between systems, so the same pump has three identifiers and no single view is possible until that is resolved.

We would settle the asset model early. It is unglamorous and it is the difference between integration and an ongoing reconciliation project.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

What happens after launch?

Most of the product life, and the part most business cases underfund.

A deployed fleet needs monitoring, incident response, firmware maintenance, certificate rotation and a plan for components going end of life. Certificates in particular expire on a date nobody diarised, and an expired fleet certificate is an outage across every device at once.

Field telemetry should return to engineering rather than to a warranty log, because that is what makes the next revision informed by evidence rather than by anecdote.

We would want the first year after launch planned as work rather than as slack, with someone owning what the fleet reports and the authority to act on it.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

Can you work with our existing engineering team?

Usually, and it is the shape that works best. Your team holds the domain, which is the part that cannot be usefully outsourced.

The common split is that you own the product and the customer relationship, and we bring the disciplines that are hard to staff for a single deployment: hardware, certification, platform engineering, test automation. Connected products need all of those briefly and none of them permanently.

What matters more than the split is who decides. Programs go wrong when ownership is ambiguous rather than when the team is mixed.

Where we build it alone, we would still expect an internal owner. A deployment with no internal owner produces a product that is technically correct and commercially wrong.

Sources: NIST cyber-physical systems framework

What is the smallest sensible engagement?

A solution read. A few weeks, a defined output, and no obligation to do anything afterward.

It covers what the deployment has to achieve, the constraints that will decide the architecture, whether custom hardware is justified, which certifications apply and what the connectivity will cost across the fleet lifetime.

The output is a written problem statement and a blueprint, structured so that do not build this is an available conclusion. Some connected product ideas do not survive an honest power budget, and finding that out in three weeks is worth considerably more than finding it in month nine.

Everything larger is a decision you can make afterward with better information than you have now.
