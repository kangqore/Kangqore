Cloud Computing, Governed Beyond the Migration Date.

Most cloud programs are funded as a migration. There is a business case, a target date, a cutover weekend, and a slide confirming the workloads arrived. Then the program closes, the team returns to other work, and the estate is left in the state it happened to be in on the day the funding stopped.

That state is rarely the one the business case described. Instances were sized for the migration rather than for steady running. Licenses came across unchanged because changing them was out of scope. Nothing was rationalized, because rationalization was the stage after the one that got funded. The saving in the business case is still theoretically available and nobody owns the work to collect it.

So we treat cloud as a cycle with four stages that keep handing to each other. Advisory establishes the case and the architecture. Transform moves and integrates the estate. Managed operations run it. Optimization rationalizes what is actually running, and what it finds becomes the next business case rather than a report nobody funds.

The practical difference is that the fourth stage exists at all. It is where the money in the original case is realized, and it is the stage that gets cut when a program is scoped as a migration with a finish line.

The fourth stage is where the business case gets paid.

Cloud Computing Services and Capabilities.

Four capabilities matching the four stages of the cycle, so wherever your program currently sits, the stage after it is already defined.

Cloud Advisory & Assessment

What you hold, what it would cost to move, and whether moving it is the right call for each individual workload.

  • Cloud maturity model: Establish where the organization actually sits on operating model, skills, governance and tooling, because a target architecture the team cannot run is a plan to be dependent on whoever built it.
  • Portfolio analysis: Assess applications against the cost and risk of moving each one, and name the ones that should be retired or left alone rather than migrated on the assumption that everything goes.
  • Profiling and runtime management: Measure what workloads genuinely consume under real load rather than what they were provisioned for, because the provisioning figure is what turns a migration into an expensive lift.
  • ROI and business case: Model the case on the estate as it will run afterward rather than as it runs today, with the licensing and rightsizing assumptions written down where they can be checked later.

Cloud Build, Deploy & Integrate

Moving the estate and rewiring what stayed behind, with the business running throughout and every cutover rehearsed first.

  • Migrate: Move workloads by the route each one warrants, rehost where that is genuinely right and re-architect where rehosting would carry a problem into a place it costs more to hold.
  • Integrate: Rewire the connections between what moved and what did not, because a hybrid estate is the normal end state rather than a transitional phase somebody will tidy up later.
  • Proof of concept: Prove the difficult workload before the wave that depends on it, so the surprise arrives while the plan can still change rather than during the cutover window.
  • Validation and deployment: Verify against the behavior the business depends on rather than against a checklist, and rehearse the rollback before anyone needs it.

Cloud Managed Services

Monitoring, maintaining and running the estate once it is there, with the routine work done by the platform rather than a person.

  • Orchestration: One control plane holding inventory and state, with the operational tooling reading from it rather than each tool maintaining its own partial copy of the truth.
  • Automation: Routine operational work executed by the platform rather than by a person following a runbook, because a runbook that only one engineer can follow is a dependency rather than a process.
  • Metering: Consumption measured and attributed to the team that can change it, so cost is a property of a workload instead of a line on a consolidated bill nobody owns.
  • Self service: Provisioning through a governed catalog, so teams move without a ticket and without routing around the controls that make the estate governable.
  • IT as a service: Operations expressed as services with owners and expectations, rather than as an undifferentiated queue that absorbs whatever arrives.

Cloud Optimization

The stage that pays for the rest, and the one most programs close down before they ever reach it.

  • Rationalize: Find what is running without a purpose, sized beyond its load, or duplicated across waves, and retire it rather than carrying it forward at every renewal.
  • Augment cloud capacity: Match capacity to the demand curve rather than to the peak, including the workloads that genuinely need the peak and should keep it.
  • Transformation projects: Turn the findings into funded work rather than a report, because an optimization finding with no owner and no budget line is an observation.

What the estate has to satisfy, sector by sector.

The engineering is much the same across these sectors. What changes is who audits your estate, what an outage costs you, and which obligations decide the architecture before any preference does.

Banking & Financial Services

The regulator decides the architecture before any cost case does.

  • Data residency and provider concentration risk settled at design
  • Exit and portability provisions demonstrable rather than asserted
  • Segregation of duties enforced through account and identity boundaries
  • Change evidence produced by the pipeline as work happens
  • Recovery objectives tested against the regulated requirement
  • Encryption and key custody held where the regulator expects it

Healthcare & Life Sciences

Patient data obligations outlive the migration that moved it.

  • Classification driving encryption, access and retention per data class
  • Masked or synthetic data in every non production environment
  • Validated change control that survives inspection years later
  • Audit trails retained for the required period without exception
  • Availability designed around clinical rather than business hours
  • Residency constraints treated as architecture rather than preference

Retail & eCommerce

Peak decides the architecture, and the other eleven months decide the bill.

  • Capacity designed for peak without paying for peak year round
  • Cardholder data scope minimized rather than migrated as it stands
  • Graceful degradation when a downstream dependency fails
  • Cost attributed per channel so trading decisions see their own spend
  • Change freezes respected by the automation, not only by the team
  • Third party and subprocessor exposure mapped deliberately

Manufacturing & Industrial

Some of the estate cannot move, and the architecture has to accept that.

  • Separation between operational technology and corporate networks preserved
  • Hybrid treated as the end state rather than a transitional phase
  • Latency sensitive workloads kept where the latency requires them
  • Long support horizons planned into the architecture from the start
  • Historian and legacy platforms integrated rather than migrated
  • Site connectivity constraints reflected in the migration sequence

Public Sector

Procurement and sovereignty shape the estate before any requirement does.

  • Data sovereignty and residency settled before provider selection
  • Open standards and exit provisions considered from the start
  • Separation of duties across delegated administrative boundaries
  • Spend transparency at the granularity the funding body expects
  • Records retention enforced through platform policy
  • Security framework mapped to the applicable national standard

Frequently Asked Questions

Our cloud bill went up after migrating. What went wrong?

Usually nothing went wrong during the migration. What went wrong is that the migration was the whole program, and the stage that collects the saving never got funded.

Workloads are sized to survive the cutover, which means generously. Licenses come across unchanged because renegotiating them was out of scope. Everything migrates, including the applications that should have been retired, because retiring things is a conversation with a business owner and moving them is not. None of that is a mistake. It is what a program optimized for a date will rationally do.

The result is an estate shaped by the move rather than by steady running, and a business case whose savings are still available but unclaimed. The work to collect them is real and it needs an owner.

So the honest answer is that the bill going up is the expected outcome of a three stage program, and the fix is the fourth stage rather than a tool.

Should everything move to cloud?

No, and a portfolio analysis that concludes everything should move has usually not been done properly.

There are workloads where the economics genuinely do not work: steady predictable load with no elasticity to exploit, licensing that prices punitively off-premises, a latency or data residency constraint that a region cannot satisfy. Moving those is a cost increase dressed as modernization.

There are also applications that should be retired rather than migrated, and they are the cheapest win in any assessment. Every estate carries some, and they survive migrations because nobody wants the conversation about switching them off.

We would rather hand you a list that includes leave alone and retire alongside move, because an assessment where every answer is move is a sales document rather than an analysis.

Sources: NIST definition of cloud computing

How do you decide rehost against re-architect?

By what the workload will cost you after it arrives, not by what it costs to move it.

Rehosting is faster and cheaper on the day. It also carries the workload forward exactly as it is, including whatever made it expensive, and in cloud some of those things cost more than they did on hardware you had already bought. A monolith that scales only vertically is one of them.

Re-architecting is the right call where the application is genuinely central, will be invested in for years, and has a shape that cloud economics punish. It is the wrong call where the application is stable, peripheral and nobody intends to change it, which describes more of most estates than teams expect.

The decision is per workload and it belongs in the assessment. A program that picks one strategy for the whole estate has made the decision by policy rather than by evidence.

Sources: AWS migration strategies

How do you stop cloud spend becoming a number nobody owns?

By attributing it to the team that can act on it, which requires tagging and account structure to be decided before workloads arrive rather than reconstructed afterward.

A consolidated bill is a number that arrives monthly and belongs to whoever gets forwarded it. Nobody on that list provisioned the largest line. Attribution turns the same number into several smaller ones, each landing with someone who can do something about it, and that alone changes behavior more than any dashboard.

Shared infrastructure is the part that gets skipped. A Kubernetes cluster serving nine teams shows as one line unless something apportions it, so that apportionment has to exist from the start.

The measure that matters is not the total. It is whether the team that could reduce a line can see it.

Multi-cloud or one provider?

One provider unless you can name the specific reason for a second, because multi-cloud has a real running cost that is usually paid for a benefit nobody collects.

The costs are concrete: every control has to be built twice, expertise splits across two platforms, data egress between them is charged, and the lowest common denominator architecture gives up the managed services that made the platform worth choosing. Teams accept those to avoid lock in, then never exercise the portability they paid for.

Good reasons exist. A regulator requiring provider diversity, an acquisition that arrived on another platform, a specific service with no equivalent, or a genuine negotiating position at renewal scale.

What we would avoid is portability as an unstated goal. If nobody can say what would trigger moving a workload, the option is costing you and will not be used.

What actually happens to our operations team?

The work changes shape rather than disappearing, and programs that assume otherwise are the ones that end up dependent on whoever ran the migration.

Patching and capacity provisioning shrink. What grows is anything to do with identity, cost, policy and automation, and those are skills the existing team is generally well placed to build because they already understand the estate and its history. That context is the expensive part to replace.

What goes badly is when a platform arrives that nobody internally can operate. The tooling is fine, the runbooks exist, and the organization is still dependent because the knowledge left with the program. We would rather build alongside your team and hand over than run it indefinitely.

If we are still essential after a year, something was designed wrong.

How long does a migration take?

Longer than the estimate, and the overrun is almost always in dependencies rather than in the moving.

The moving is the predictable part. What is not predictable is discovering that a workload nobody documented talks to four others, that a licensing term prohibits the target, or that the application owner left two years ago and nobody can approve a cutover window. Those surface during migration if they were not found during assessment, which is why assessment is worth doing properly.

So we would rather give you a sequence than a date: which waves, what has to be true before each, and which dependency is the one most likely to move everything. That is a more useful commitment than a date built on assumptions nobody has tested.

The workloads that break the schedule are usually known. Somebody in the room already suspects which they are.

Sources: AWS migration strategies

What is a landing zone and do we actually need one?

It is account structure, identity, network and guardrails provisioned before workloads arrive. You need one, and the only real question is whether you build it first or retrofit it later at considerably greater cost.

Without it, each team provisions what it needs and the estate accretes. Boundaries end up wherever a project put them, permissions are broad because narrowing them later breaks something, and nobody can answer which accounts hold regulated data. Every one of those is cheap to set and expensive to change once workloads depend on them.

With it, guardrails are configuration. A workload lands in an account whose boundaries were decided before it, rather than in one adjusted afterward to accept it.

Retrofitting is a real project. We have never seen an estate where doing it first was the more expensive choice.

Sources: AWS Well-Architected Framework

How do you keep the running estate matching the approved architecture?

By defining infrastructure as code and comparing the running estate against it continuously, so divergence is detected rather than discovered.

Drift is never a decision. Someone makes a console change during an incident, entirely correctly, and it never gets back into the definition. Repeat that across a year and the code describes an estate that no longer exists, which means the next deployment from it is unpredictable and nobody trusts it enough to run.

What prevents it is making the code the easier path and detection automatic. A drift report that arrives weekly and goes to a distribution list is not detection. It has to land with the team that owns the resource, and reconciling it has to be routine rather than a project.

The estates that stay clean are the ones where the emergency console change is expected and the path back into code is short.

Sources: AWS Well-Architected Framework

Can you take over a cloud estate somebody else built?

Yes, and it is a common shape. The first stage is always the same: establish what is actually running, because the documentation and the estate have usually diverged.

We would start with discovery rather than with recommendations. Inventory, dependency mapping, cost attribution, posture, and where the running estate departs from whatever was designed. That takes a few weeks and it routinely finds workloads nobody could name an owner for.

What we would not do is recommend rebuilding it. Inherited estates attract that advice and it is usually wrong. Most of what is there works, and the valuable work is narrower: the parts that cost too much, the parts that cannot be reasoned about, and the parts that would fail an audit.

You would get a written position on what to leave alone, which is generally the largest category.

Sources: AWS Well-Architected Framework

What does managed service actually cover?

Monitoring, incident response, patching, backup verification, capacity, cost reporting and a governed self service catalog. What matters more than the list is where the boundary sits and who decides what.

The common failure is ambiguity. An incident occurs, and whether the fix is ours or yours is not written down, so it is argued while the incident continues. We would want that agreed explicitly at the start, including which changes we make without asking and which are escalations.

The second thing worth settling is what happens to improvement work. A managed service that only keeps the lights on will hold an estate exactly as it was handed over, including the parts that should change. Optimization has to be inside the arrangement or it does not happen.

We would also rather you could leave. Runbooks and infrastructure code stay yours.

Sources: AWS Well-Architected Framework

What is the smallest sensible engagement?

A cloud assessment. A few weeks, a defined output, and no obligation to do anything afterward.

It covers what is running, what it depends on, what it actually consumes against what it was provisioned for, where the cost concentrates, and which applications should move, be retired or be left alone. For an existing estate it also covers where the running state has drifted from the approved architecture.

The output is a written position and a sequence, structured so that leave it alone is an available conclusion for any individual workload and for the estate as a whole. Some estates are in better shape than their owners assume, and finding that out is worth the few weeks.

Everything larger is a decision you can make afterward with better information than you have now.
