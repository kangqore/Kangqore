Managed Services, Built to Change What They Run.

Every program before this one ended with a handover. The migration completed, the platform shipped, the security work passed its audit, and somebody signed the closure note. Then it became a running estate, and running is where the numbers in the original business case are either collected or quietly written off.

Most run contracts are priced to keep things exactly as they were handed over. Tickets closed inside the agreed window, uptime held, patches applied on schedule. That is a real service and it is also the reason estates stay precisely as expensive as the day they were inherited. Nothing in the arrangement pays anybody to make them cheaper, simpler or smaller.

So improvement sits inside the arrangement rather than beside it. The team running your estate is the team that reports what it costs you, owns the automation backlog, and is measured on the tickets that stopped arriving rather than the tickets it closed. What that team finds becomes funded work instead of a note in a quarterly review.

The difference shows up in the second year. A service that only holds the estate looks identical in month twenty four to month one. A service that improves it should be running a visibly smaller, cheaper and quieter thing than the one you handed over.

Managed Services and Capabilities.

Five services you can take separately or together, each run so the estate it holds gets better rather than merely stays up.

Managed Data, AI and Automation

Getting your data ready to be useful, then keeping it that way once the pilots become production.

  • Data foundations: Most AI pilots stall because the data underneath them was never fit for it, so the work starts with what you hold, where it came from and who is allowed to see it.
  • Managed data platform operations: Pipelines, quality checks and lineage run as a service, so a broken feed is caught by the platform rather than reported by whoever noticed the number looked wrong.
  • Reusable data products: Trusted, documented and reusable rather than rebuilt per project, which is what makes the second use case faster than the first.
  • Managed AI operations: Models monitored in production for drift and cost, with an owner named for each one, because a model nobody owns is a decision nobody can explain.
  • Intelligent automation: Routine operational work executed by the platform, chosen by what it removes from the queue rather than by what demonstrates well.

Global Capability Centers

Standing up and running a capability center that earns its place beyond cost arbitrage.

  • Center design and stand up: Location, legal entity, operating model and the roles that genuinely belong there rather than the ones that were cheapest to move.
  • Talent and capability building: Access to skills that are hard to hire locally, with a path from delivery work to ownership so the center does not stay junior by design.
  • Operating model and governance: How work reaches the center, who decides, and what escalates, written down before the first team lands rather than negotiated afterward.
  • Transition and knowledge transfer: Work moved deliberately with the context that makes it possible to do well, because a transition that moves tasks without knowledge moves the problem.
  • From delivery to influence: A center measured on the outcomes it moves rather than the tickets it absorbs, which is the difference between a cost line and a capability.

Application Managed Services

Running your application estate while reducing what there is to run.

  • Application support and maintenance: Incident, problem and change across the estate, with the support tiers defined so an issue reaches the people who can actually resolve it.
  • Complexity reduction: An estate gets more complex every year unless somebody is paid to make it simpler, so retirement and consolidation are inside the service rather than a separate project.
  • Enhancement delivery: Small changes delivered continuously against the same pipeline as support work, so a business request does not wait for a release train it was never on.
  • Technical debt paydown: A funded share of capacity spent on the things that generate tickets, chosen from what the ticket data actually says rather than from what the team dislikes most.
  • Vendor and estate coordination: Third party applications and their suppliers managed as part of the estate, because the boundary is where accountability usually disappears.

Infrastructure Managed Services

Stabilize, optimize and transform the infrastructure you have, at the pace it can absorb.

  • Operationalize: Automation introduced to reduce cost and raise quality, building the foundation for an operating model that can run hybrid and cloud at different speeds without pretending they are the same.
  • Accelerate: Infrastructure running more efficiently and more securely, freeing funding that was being spent holding the estate still.
  • Modernize: The landscape re-engineered continually rather than in one program, with operations and skills reassessed as the estate changes underneath them.
  • Monitoring and response: Proactive observability across the estate, so the first person who knows about an outage works for us rather than for you.
  • Cost and capacity management: Consumption measured and attributed to the team that can change it, because a consolidated bill belongs to nobody.

Cybersecurity Managed Services

Security run as an operational service, aligned to what the business can actually lose.

  • Cyber risk strategy: Controls prioritized by what they protect and what its loss would cost, rather than by the order a framework happens to list them in.
  • Managed detection and response: Monitoring, threat hunting and incident response against a correlated view, with data from on premises and cloud landing in one place rather than three consoles.
  • Vulnerability and posture management: Findings ranked by reachability in your architecture, because a critical in something nothing can reach matters less than a medium on your authentication path.
  • Identity and access operations: Joiners, movers and leavers executed as a process rather than as a ticket, since access nobody revoked is the most common way a breach becomes a bad one.
  • Resilience and recovery: Recovery rehearsed against the objectives the business actually signed up to, because a plan nobody has run is a document rather than a capability.

What the service has to satisfy, by function and by sector.

Managed services run across business functions and regulated sectors alike. What changes is who audits the work, what an error costs you, and which obligations decide the arrangement before any preference does.

Finance operations

Every number you publish has to be defensible months after it was produced.

  • Record to report, order to cash and procure to pay run to an agreed calendar
  • Period close supported without the month end heroics that hide a process problem
  • Segregation of duties preserved across the provider boundary
  • Control evidence produced as work happens rather than gathered for the auditor
  • Exceptions worked to root cause, because the same reconciliation break recurring is a defect
  • Intercompany, tax and statutory variation handled per entity rather than per exception

Supply chain

The process only matters on the day something upstream goes wrong.

  • Demand and supply planning cycles run to the cadence the business actually plans on
  • Exception handling defined before disruption, not improvised during it
  • Inventory and order accuracy measured against what the warehouse holds, not the system
  • Supplier and carrier data kept current, since stale master data causes most failures
  • Escalation paths that reach a person with authority inside the shipping window
  • Seasonal capacity arranged before the season rather than during it

Sourcing & procurement

Savings signed in a contract are not savings until someone operates them.

  • Source to contract and contract to pay run as one process rather than two teams
  • Supplier onboarding with the due diligence the risk category actually warrants
  • Contracted rates enforced at the invoice rather than reviewed annually
  • Maverick spend visible to the budget holder who can stop it
  • Category data maintained so spend analysis answers questions rather than raising them
  • Supplier risk monitored continuously where concentration or geography justifies it

Digital engineering & manufacturing

Support that reaches the plant floor carries consequences an office system does not.

  • Separation between operational technology and corporate networks preserved
  • Maintenance windows aligned to production schedules rather than to ours
  • Engineering application support that understands the tolerance behind the ticket
  • Long support horizons for equipment that outlives its vendor
  • Change control on systems where a bad release stops a line
  • Quality and traceability records retained to the standard the sector inspects against

Talent and HR

The data is about people, and mistakes in it are felt personally.

  • Hire to retire processes run with the accuracy payroll depends on
  • Employee data handled under the privacy rules of each jurisdiction it sits in
  • Joiners, movers and leavers executed as a process rather than as a ticket
  • Payroll cutoffs treated as immovable, because a missed one is somebody unpaid
  • Case management with escalation paths a manager can actually use
  • Access to sensitive records restricted by role and audited without exception

Marketing Services

Campaign operations fail on the boring parts, not the creative ones.

  • Campaign build, trafficking and quality assurance run to the launch calendar
  • Consent and preference honored across every channel that touches a customer
  • Martech stack administered so the team spends its time on the work, not the tooling
  • Asset and brand governance applied without becoming a bottleneck
  • Reporting that attributes outcomes rather than counting activity
  • Peak trading periods staffed and frozen deliberately rather than hopefully

Customer Service Operations

Every contact is a person who already has a problem.

  • Omnichannel handling with context carried across the channel switch
  • Quality measured on resolution and effort rather than on handle time alone
  • Automation applied where it genuinely removes a contact, not where it deflects one
  • Escalation to a human that works on the first attempt
  • Workforce planning matched to the demand curve rather than to an average
  • Customer data accessed under the same controls as any other regulated record

Banking

The regulator has opinions about who runs your systems.

  • Outsourcing and concentration risk evidenced to the regulator
  • Right to audit and exit provisions demonstrable rather than asserted
  • Segregation of duties preserved across the provider boundary
  • Change evidence produced as work happens rather than assembled later
  • Recovery tested against regulated objectives rather than internal targets
  • Access recertification covering provider personnel as well as your own

Insurance

Claims and policy work is where the promise is either kept or broken.

  • Claims handled to service standards the policyholder was actually sold
  • Policy administration accuracy checked against the document, not the record
  • Underwriting support that respects the appetite rather than the volume target
  • Regulatory reporting produced from the source rather than reconstructed
  • Vulnerable customer handling defined as process rather than left to judgment
  • Fraud signals routed to investigation without stalling legitimate claims

Health

Patient data obligations do not transfer with the work.

  • Data classification driving what a provider may see and from where
  • Masked or synthetic data in every non production environment
  • Validated change control that survives inspection years later
  • Audit trails retained for the required period without exception
  • Availability designed around clinical hours rather than business hours
  • Incident notification thresholds agreed before an incident, not during one

Risk and Compliance

Controls that are only tested annually are assumptions for the other eleven months.

  • Control testing run continuously where the evidence can be collected automatically
  • Regulatory change tracked against the controls it actually affects
  • Know your customer and anti money laundering operations run to the required standard
  • Issue and remediation tracking with owners and dates that mean something
  • Findings ranked by exposure rather than by the order a framework lists them
  • Evidence retained so an examination is a lookup rather than a project

Trust & safety

The work protects users, and it has to protect the people doing it too.

  • Policy enforcement applied consistently and measured for consistency
  • Escalation paths for material that needs specialist or legal handling
  • Reviewer wellbeing designed into rota, exposure limits and support
  • Appeals handled as a real process rather than as a formality
  • Automation used to reduce human exposure to the worst material first
  • Regional and legal variation respected rather than flattened into one policy

Frequently Asked Questions

How do we stop a managed service freezing our estate?

By paying for improvement explicitly, because a contract that only pays for availability will rationally deliver only availability.

The standard arrangement measures tickets closed inside a window and uptime held. Both are worth measuring and neither rewards anyone for making the estate smaller. A provider that automates a recurring incident away has reduced its own billable volume, so unless the agreement accounts for that, nobody does it twice.

What changes it is naming improvement as a deliverable with capacity attached: a funded share of effort spent on the things generating tickets, chosen from the ticket data rather than from preference, reported alongside the availability numbers.

The measure worth watching is not tickets closed. It is tickets that stopped arriving.

Ask any prospective provider what they removed for their last client rather than what they maintained. The answer is usually revealing, because most cannot name anything: the contract never asked them to.

Can you take over from our current provider?

Yes, and the transition is the part that decides whether the arrangement works. Most run engagements that fail, failed here.

The risk is never the tooling. It is knowledge: the undocumented workaround, the system whose owner left, the process that works because one person remembers the exception. A transition that moves tickets without moving that context has moved the problem and added a handover to it.

So we would want overlap with the outgoing party, waves rather than a single date, and reverse shadowing where our people run the work while theirs are still reachable. That costs more up front and it is cheaper than the alternative.

We would also expect to find things nobody knew were running. Every estate has them, and discovery is where they surface rather than during your first incident.

The honest timeline is longer than most proposals suggest. A transition rushed to hit a contract start date produces months of instability afterward, and that instability is invariably read as the new provider being worse than the old one.

Sources: ITIL service management framework

What happens to our existing team?

The work changes shape rather than disappearing, and programs that assume otherwise end up dependent on whoever ran the transition.

Routine operational work shrinks. What grows is anything to do with vendor management, architecture, cost and the decisions about what should exist at all. Your people hold the context that makes those decisions well, and that context is the expensive part to replace.

What goes badly is a service arriving that nobody internally understands. The runbooks exist, the reports arrive, and the organization has quietly lost the ability to judge whether any of it is good.

We would rather your team keeps the judgment and we take the volume. If you cannot challenge our reporting after a year, something was designed wrong.

How is this priced?

Per service, against a defined scope, with the improvement capacity named separately so it cannot quietly become the first thing cut.

The pricing question that matters more than the rate is what happens when volume falls. If the fee is per ticket or per device, a shrinking estate reduces your bill and ours, which is aligned. If it is a fixed fee, the incentive runs the other way once automation starts working, and that needs addressing in the agreement rather than in good intentions.

We would rather the arrangement be explicit about it than discover the misalignment in year two.

What we avoid is pricing that rewards absorbing more work. It is the most common shape and it is why estates grow under management.

Sources: FinOps Foundation framework

What is a global capability center, and do we need one?

It is your own team in another location, rather than a supplier. You need one when the work is continuous, strategic and large enough that you want to own the capability rather than rent it.

The case is weaker than it looks below a certain scale. A center carries entity setup, compliance, leadership and attrition, and those costs do not scale down gracefully. Below roughly the size where you would hire a leadership layer anyway, a managed service is usually cheaper and considerably faster.

The case is strong where the knowledge is the asset. Work that takes a year to learn is work you do not want to re-transition every time a contract ends.

Where centers disappoint is when they are set up to cut cost and then measured on cost, which guarantees they stay at execution and never reach ownership.

Sources: ITIL service management framework

Our AI pilots are not reaching production. Is that a data problem?

Usually, and more specifically an ownership problem that shows up as a data problem.

A pilot works on a curated extract that somebody assembled by hand. Production needs that extract to arrive daily, correctly, with someone accountable when it does not. That is an operational commitment nobody made during the pilot, so the model waits for a platform that was never scoped.

The other half is trust. Business users will not act on an output they cannot trace, so lineage and a named owner matter more to adoption than model accuracy does past a certain point.

So we would look at what it takes to run the inputs as a service before adding more models. The pilots you already have are usually enough to justify that work.

How do you handle a serious security incident?

Against a plan that has been rehearsed, with the decision rights agreed before the incident rather than during it.

The technical response is the part most organizations have. What they lack is the agreed answer to who can take a production system offline, who speaks to a regulator, who tells customers and at what threshold. Those questions are answered badly at three in the morning.

So we would want an incident response plan with named roles on both sides, exercised at least annually against a realistic scenario, including the communications path. The exercise finds the gaps; the document only records them.

And we would tell you early rather than when the picture is complete. A provider that manages the narrative before informing you is managing the wrong risk.

Sources: NIST Computer Security Incident Handling Guide

How deep does application support actually go?

As deep as the agreement says, and the important part is that the boundary is written down rather than assumed.

The common shape runs from service desk through operational fixes to code level change for applications we support directly. Where it usually gets ambiguous is third party software, where the honest answer is that we manage the vendor and the incident, and the fix is theirs.

The failure mode is an incident nobody owns while two parties discuss whose it is. We would want that written into the arrangement, including the escalation path when a vendor is unresponsive.

Enhancement work is worth settling too. If small changes are outside the scope, they queue behind a release train and the business stops asking.

Sources: ITIL service management framework

What do you actually report?

What it cost, what broke, what changed, and what stopped happening. The last one is the only part that shows whether the arrangement is working.

Availability and ticket volumes are table stakes and they describe activity. A service improving your estate should be able to show cost per unit falling, recurring incident classes disappearing, and the count of things it runs going down rather than up.

We would rather report a number that went the wrong way with an explanation than present a dashboard where everything is green. Green dashboards are usually a reporting choice.

And the reporting should be readable by someone who does not work in operations. If it needs interpretation, it is not reporting.

Sources: FinOps Foundation framework

Can we take one of these services without the others?

Yes. They are separate services and most engagements start with one.

What you get by taking more than one is a single front door, one configuration record and one report, which matters more than it sounds. The most common operational failure at a boundary is two providers each correctly doing their part while an incident sits between them.

Where a single service is clearly right, we would say so. Infrastructure and security together are a natural pair; data and applications are frequently better separate because they answer to different parts of your business.

We would rather run one service well than five adequately.

Sources: ITIL service management framework

What happens if it is not working?

You should be able to leave, and the arrangement should be built so leaving is possible rather than theoretical.

That means runbooks, infrastructure code, configuration data and ticket history are yours throughout and not held as leverage. It means no undocumented dependency on a tool we own. And it means an exit period long enough to transition properly, agreed at the start when nobody is annoyed.

Before that, there should be a review point with real consequences. A quarterly meeting where the numbers are examined by someone empowered to change the arrangement, not a status call.

A provider confident in the service will agree to all of this readily. Reluctance here is information.

Sources: ITIL service management framework

What is the smallest sensible engagement?

An operations read. A few weeks, a defined output, no obligation afterward.

It covers what you run, what it costs, what generates your tickets, where the knowledge is concentrated in individuals, and which parts of the estate nobody can name an owner for. For an existing managed service it also covers whether you are being charged to hold something that should have been retired.

The output is a written position and a sequence, structured so that keep it in house is an available conclusion. Sometimes the finding is that your team is doing fine and the problem is tooling.

Everything larger is a decision you can make afterward with better information than you have now.

What it will not do is tell you we should run everything. Some of what you hold is genuinely better kept close, and a read that concludes otherwise about every single service was a sales document rather than an assessment.
