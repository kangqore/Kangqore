Enterprise AWS, Built for the Data That Runs on It.

Two things decide whether an AWS estate pays for itself. What you are able to offer a customer at the moment they are deciding, and what you are paying to run the estate that offers it. Both are usually settled long before anyone examines them, by a license position inherited at renewal and an identity model assembled by whichever team needed it first.

Personalization is the clearest example. Building a recommendation engine used to be the expensive part, and it no longer is. Amazon Personalize automates most of the model work, which moves the difficulty somewhere less obvious: a managed model trained on an unresolved customer will produce confident recommendations for a person who is three separate records in your systems. The model is not wrong. The data underneath it is, and the model has no way to say so.

Cost behaves the same way. What an estate costs is largely decided by license entitlements, instance families and storage tiers chosen years ago and carried forward at every renewal since. Those are all examinable, and examining them before a migration is considerably cheaper than renegotiating after one, when the workloads are placed and the leverage has gone.

So we treat the landing zone, the data platform and the intelligence on top of it as one engagement. Identity and consent live in the platform rather than in whichever tool captured them first, and a metric has one definition that both the batch and streaming paths read. The result is not a longer program. It is the same program without the reconciliation project that usually follows it.

The segment means the same thing in every system that reads it.

  • One Profile Across Every Channel. Whether a customer is one resolved profile across web, app, store and support, or a different record in each of them.
  • One Definition Batch And Streaming. Whether the batch path and the streaming path compute a number the same way, or produce two answers that each get defended.
  • Traced Not Trusted Every Model In Production. Whether a prediction can be traced to the data and code that produced it, long after the person who trained it has moved on.
  • Before Not After Where Controls Apply. When guardrails are applied. Before data lands it is configuration, afterward it is a migration with a compliance deadline attached.

What the data has to prove, sector by sector.

The platform engineering is much the same across these sectors. What changes is who audits the model, what a wrong answer costs, and which obligations are not negotiable.

Banking & Financial Services

A model that affects a customer outcome has to be explainable to someone paid to doubt it.

  • Model decisions traceable to the data and version that produced them
  • Bias testing across protected characteristics, evidenced and retained
  • Segregation between the team that builds a model and the team that approves it
  • Data lineage from the report back to the system of record
  • Encryption and key custody demonstrable rather than asserted
  • Retention aligned to the regulator rather than to storage cost

Retail & eCommerce

Identity resolution and consent decide what the analytics are allowed to do.

  • One resolved customer across web, app, store and support systems
  • Consent captured once and enforced wherever a segment is activated
  • Recommendation quality measured on outcome rather than click through alone
  • Peak season capacity designed without paying for it all year
  • Partner data collaboration without exchanging raw customer records
  • Churn signals surfaced early enough that intervention is still possible

Healthcare & Life Sciences

Patient data carries obligations that outlive the project that created the platform.

  • Classification driving encryption, access and retention per data class
  • Masked or synthetic data in every non production environment
  • Access limited by role and by clinical context rather than role alone
  • Validated change control that survives inspection years later
  • Model performance reported by cohort, not as a single headline figure
  • Audit trails retained for the required period without exception

Manufacturing & Industrial

The data arrives from equipment that was never designed to produce it.

  • Ingestion from operational technology without bridging it to corporate networks
  • Streaming paths that tolerate intermittent and constrained connectivity
  • Image and video inspection models evaluated on your own defects
  • Historian data reconciled with modern platforms rather than replaced
  • Edge inference where latency or bandwidth makes the round trip impractical
  • Long support horizons planned into the architecture from the start

Media & Entertainment

Recommendation and rights are the same problem viewed from two directions.

  • Content metadata enriched automatically rather than cataloged by hand
  • Recommendation that respects territory and rights windows by design
  • Audio and video classification across an archive nobody has time to watch
  • Audience segmentation that survives a household with several viewers
  • Cost per stream understood at the point the architecture is chosen
  • Subtitle, transcript and accessibility generation built into ingestion

Modeled scenario in retail, multi channel. 6 → 1 definitions of an active customer

Six teams each had a defensible definition of an active customer, built into the tool that needed it first. Campaign counts never matched the warehouse, so every review spent its first half arguing about whose number was right rather than what to do about it.

Identity resolution moved onto AWS Entity Resolution, with the definition of an active customer held once in the AWS Glue Data Catalog and read by Amazon Redshift and the campaign tool alike. The number stopped being contested, and segment work moved from reconciliation to targeting.

Frequently Asked Questions

We are already on AWS. Where does this start?

With what the data is allowed to do, not with what the platform can do. That is the constraint set that decides the architecture, and it is the one that rarely arrives written down.

Being on AWS already tells us about compute and storage. It says nothing about whether accounts are separated in a way that survives an audit, whether personal data has a classification that drives encryption and retention, or whether the definition of a metric exists anywhere outside the query that produced it.

So the first stage looks at the account structure, the data that already exists and the decisions the business wants to improve. It usually finds that the platform is sound and the governance around it was written last, which is a cheaper problem than the reverse.

The output is a constraint list, a target architecture and a sequence. It is deliberately unglamorous, and it is the part that stops the reconciliation project from arriving later.

Sources: AWS Well-Architected Framework

What does a customer 360 actually require from us?

An agreement on what counts as the same person, and a decision about who owns that answer. The engineering is the straightforward half.

Identity resolution is a policy question wearing a technical costume. Is a shared household email one customer or two? Does a lapsed account merge with a new signup at the same address? Every organization has a defensible answer and most have several, held by different teams, each built into the tool that needed it first.

What we need from you is the authority to settle it once, and access to the systems that hold the identifiers. What you get back is a resolved profile that segmentation, recommendation and churn work all read from, rather than three teams each maintaining their own.

The uncomfortable part is usually that the first resolved count is lower than the numbers people were reporting. That is the point of doing it.

Sources: AWS Entity Resolution

Real time or batch? How do we decide?

By asking whether anything actually changes if the answer arrives sooner. If the decision it feeds is made weekly, streaming buys latency nobody spends.

Real time earns its cost where the window to act is short: fraud, abandonment, an operational alert, a recommendation inside a live session. Where it rarely earns it is reporting, where the audience reads the number once a day and a nightly load is both cheaper and easier to reason about.

The expensive mistake is not choosing wrong. It is running both paths with separate logic, so the streaming number and the batch number disagree and each has a team willing to defend it. We would rather one definition is shared by both paths, even when that constrains the design.

Most platforms we build end up mixed, and that is fine. What matters is that the split was decided rather than inherited.

Sources: Amazon Managed Service for Apache Flink

How do you keep AWS cost from becoming the story?

By making cost a property of the workload rather than a monthly surprise, and by attributing it to the team that can actually change it.

Data platforms have a characteristic cost shape. Storage is cheap and grows quietly, query is expensive and spiky, and the largest line is usually something nobody meant to leave running. Untagged accounts make all three invisible until the bill consolidates them into one number that no single team owns.

So tagging and account structure come from the landing zone stage, not from a cleanup later. Lifecycle policies move cold data without anyone filing a ticket. Query patterns that scan a whole table get caught in review rather than in the invoice.

The other half is the licensing position, and it is the half that rarely gets opened. Entitlements for commercial databases and operating systems are often carried forward unchanged from an on-premises agreement, priced for hardware that no longer exists. Reviewing what you are actually entitled to, before workloads are placed, routinely changes the shape of a migration.

We would rather show you the cost of a design decision while it is still a decision. After it is built, cost optimization is a project competing with everything else for time.

We want a recommendation engine. Do we build it or use Amazon Personalize?

Use Personalize unless you can name the reason it will not fit. The model stopped being the hard part some time ago, and building one yourself now buys maintenance rather than accuracy.

Personalize automates most of what a recommendation team used to do by hand: algorithm selection, training, tuning and serving the result at request latency. For related items, personalized ranking and catalog recommendation it reaches a good result faster than a team starting from a notebook, and it keeps working after that team moves on to something else.

What it cannot do is fix its inputs. A managed model trained on an unresolved customer produces confident recommendations for someone who is three records in your systems, and nothing in the output indicates that is what happened. Cold start is the other place it needs a decision rather than a default: what a new customer or a newly listed item is shown before there is any behavior to rank on.

Custom earns its place where the ranking problem is genuinely yours, such as a catalog carrying constraints Personalize does not model, rights windows, live inventory or a regulated exclusion. We would evaluate both on your data before committing, because that comparison changes the answer more often than people expect.

Sources: Amazon Personalize

Do we need our own models, or are the managed AI services enough?

Managed services are enough more often than teams expect, and the honest test is whether your data is genuinely different from what the service was trained on.

For transcription, document extraction, translation, general object detection and most text classification, a managed service will beat a model you train yourself, because the training set behind it is larger than anything you hold. Building your own there buys a maintenance burden and no accuracy.

Custom models earn their place where the vocabulary is yours: domain specific documents, an industrial defect nobody else photographs, a ranking problem defined by your catalog. That is also where a managed service will quietly underperform in a way a demonstration will not reveal.

The way to settle it is to evaluate both on your own material before committing. That is a fortnight of work and it has changed the answer often enough that we treat it as standard rather than optional.

Sources: Amazon Bedrock

Our data is spread across accounts and regions. Is that a blocker?

Not usually, and it is often deliberate. What matters is whether the spread is a design or an accident, because the two behave very differently under audit.

Data that sits in a region because a regulator requires it is an architecture. Data that sits in four accounts because four projects each provisioned their own is a reconciliation problem that will surface the first time somebody asks for a complete view of a customer.

We work with the boundaries rather than against them. Catalogs and permissions can be centralized while storage stays local, and there are reasonable patterns for analytics across a boundary that do not involve copying raw records over it.

What we will not do is quietly relocate data to make an architecture simpler. If a residency constraint makes the design harder, the design gets harder.

Sources: AWS shared responsibility model

How do you handle personal data in analytics and AI?

Classification first, then the controls follow from it automatically rather than being decided per project.

The pattern that causes trouble is a copy of production data in a development account so somebody could test something. It is almost never malicious and it is the most common finding we see. Masked or synthetic data in non production removes the entire category of risk, and it costs far less than the incident it prevents.

In analytics specifically, consent state has to travel with the profile rather than living in the campaign tool that first captured it. A segment that cannot evidence its lawful basis is a segment that cannot be activated, and that check belongs in the platform rather than in somebody remembering.

For AI work the same rule applies to training data. Where data cannot leave an account, the model comes to the data rather than the reverse.

Sources: AWS Lake Formation fine-grained access control

What stops the platform becoming another silo nobody trusts?

Trust follows from whether people can see where a number came from, and it collapses the first time two reports disagree and nobody can explain why.

So lineage and the definition of a metric are platform features rather than documentation. If a chart cannot tell you which table it read and how the number was computed, it will eventually be disbelieved by someone who has a different number, and rebuilding that trust costs more than publishing lineage would have.

The second failure is access. A platform that requires a ticket for every question gets routed around, and the workarounds become the next generation of silos. Fine grained permissions exist precisely so access can be broad without being unsafe.

We would rather ship fewer datasets that people rely on than a catalog of tables nobody is confident enough to use.

Sources: AWS Glue Data Catalog

How is model accuracy actually measured here?

On a held out set built from your material, against the decision the model is supposed to improve rather than against a generic score.

A headline accuracy figure is close to meaningless on its own. A model that is right ninety five percent of the time is excellent or useless depending entirely on what the other cases cost, and on whether the errors fall evenly or concentrate on one group of customers.

So we agree the evaluation before the model is built: which cases matter, what a false positive costs compared with a false negative, and which slices get reported separately. That framing tends to change the model choice, which is the point of doing it first.

After deployment the same set becomes the monitored baseline. Accuracy that is only measured once is a claim about the past.

Sources: NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Can you work with our existing data team?

Usually, and it is the shape that works best. Your team holds the domain knowledge, which is the part that cannot be usefully outsourced.

The common split is that we bring the platform engineering and the governance disciplines that are hard to staff for a single program, and your team keeps the definitions, the relationships with the source system owners and the judgment about what a number actually means. We would expect to hand over rather than to stay.

What matters more than the split is who owns the decisions. Programs go wrong when ownership is ambiguous, not when the team is mixed. We would want that written down at the start, including which calls are ours and which are escalations.

Where we build it alone, we would still expect someone internal to own the definitions. A platform with no internal owner is technically correct and commercially inert.

Sources: AWS Well-Architected Framework

What happens to a model after it goes live?

It gets an owner, a monitored baseline and a retirement path, or it should not go live. Most of the real cost of machine learning sits after deployment.

Models degrade quietly. The world moves, the input distribution shifts, and nothing throws an error. The failure surfaces as a slow decline in the outcome the model was supposed to improve, which is exactly the signal nobody is watching because the dashboard says the endpoint is healthy.

So monitoring covers the inputs and the outputs, not just the infrastructure. Drift alerts go to the team that owns the model rather than to a shared inbox. Retraining is a scheduled decision with a criterion attached, not a response to somebody noticing.

The retirement path matters as much. A model nobody can turn off because three systems quietly depend on it is a liability that grows.

Sources: Amazon SageMaker Model Monitor

What is the smallest sensible engagement?

An architecture and data read. A few weeks, a defined output, and no obligation to do anything else afterward.

It covers the account structure, how data moves today, where personal data sits, what the definitions of your core metrics actually are, and which of the decisions you want to improve are genuinely reachable with the data you hold. Some are not, and finding that out early is worth more than a proof of concept that quietly assumes otherwise.

The output is a written position: what is already sound and should be left alone, what needs to change before anything is built on it, and what the sequence would be. It is structured so that "this is fine, carry on" is an available conclusion, because sometimes it is the right one.

Everything larger is a decision you can make afterward with better information than you have now.
