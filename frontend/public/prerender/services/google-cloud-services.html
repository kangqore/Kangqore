Google Cloud, Adopted for the Reason You Chose It.

Almost nobody picks Google Cloud generically. Something specific drove it. A data estate that had outgrown its warehouse and needed BigQuery. A container platform, on the cloud that originated Kubernetes. Gemini and Vertex, because the model work had to sit next to the data rather than across an egress charge from it. The decision is usually narrow, technical and well founded.

What happens after it is where the value is won or lost. The workload that justified the choice lands and works. Then the rest of the estate follows on a generic lift and shift, because that is the fastest path and nobody has time to ask which parts should be treated differently. Eighteen months later the organization is paying hyperscaler prices for commodity infrastructure, and the capability that justified the whole move is still confined to the one team that asked for it.

So we sequence the estate around the reason rather than around the migration wave. The landing zone is built to let more than one team reach that capability safely. Applications are modernized where reaching it requires it and rehosted where it does not. Data, security and the workplace are moved in the order that makes the original case pay rather than in the order that is easiest to schedule.

The difference shows up in who is using the thing you bought. A platform decision that stays inside one project was an expensive way to fund a single team.

The capability you bought reaches more than one team.

  • Shared Not Siloed Who Can Reach It. Whether the capability that justified moving to Google Cloud is available across the organization or confined to the team that asked for it.
  • Chosen Not Default How Workloads Moved. Whether each workload moved by the route it warranted, or every workload took the route that was fastest to schedule.
  • Identity Not Network What Grants Access. Whether access is decided by identity and device posture, or by whether a request arrived from inside the network perimeter.
  • Beside Not Across Where Compute Sits. Whether analytics and model work sit beside the data, or on the other side of an egress charge that quietly caps how often they run.

Data and AI services

The data platform first, then the analytics and the models that sit on top of it, which are only ever as trustworthy as it is.

  • Database migration and modernization: Move the data layer with the downtime the business can actually absorb, and take the opportunity to leave behind what the old engine forced rather than carrying it across.
  • Smart analytics: A warehouse and lakehouse where storage and compute scale independently, with the definition of a metric held once rather than reimplemented in each tool that needed it.
  • Cloud based AI: Vision, speech, document and language capability assembled from managed services first, and built custom only where your material is genuinely unlike what the service was trained on.
  • Generative AI: Retrieval grounded in your own corpus, with evaluation agreed before the model is chosen and the refusal case designed rather than left to whatever the default returns.
  • Governance and lineage: Where a number came from, who may see it, and which model version produced a given decision, answerable months later rather than reconstructed from memory.

Application modernization

Cloud native where it genuinely earns its place, and honest rehosting for the applications nobody intends to change again.

  • Legacy assessment: Establish which applications are genuinely central and will be invested in, because those are the only ones where refactoring returns more than it costs.
  • Containerization and cloud native build: Microservices and event driven architecture where the shape of the application genuinely calls for it, on Kubernetes rather than around it.
  • Release engineering: Build, test and deploy pipelines that produce the same artifact every time and refuse a change that fails a gate, so modernization does not trade one fragility for another.
  • API management: Assessment, architecture, governance and reliability for the interfaces that outlive the applications behind them, because an API contract is harder to change than the service implementing it.
  • Quality and security in the pipeline: Static analysis, dependency scanning and policy evaluation running against every change rather than as a review before launch.

Workspace services on Google Cloud

Collaboration moved deliberately, with the identity, retention and change management that most migrations underestimate.

  • Assessment: What is in use today, what integrates with it, and what the identity, access and retention policies actually are, which is usually the part nobody has written down.
  • Migration and transformation: Move mail, files and collaboration with the history intact and the regulatory retention obligations carried across rather than quietly dropped.
  • Automation: Applications and workflows built on the low code platform, repetitive tasks scripted, and the Workspace APIs used to integrate rather than to export.
  • Identity and access: Single sign on, device policy and context aware access agreed as part of the move rather than tightened afterward once people have settled into workarounds.
  • Adoption and reliability: Self service capability and change management, because a migration everyone routes around has moved the license and not the work.

Security services on Google Cloud

Secure by design through the migration itself, rather than assessed once the estate already depends on the boundaries.

  • Consulting and advisory: Risk based assessment across infrastructure and workloads, with benchmarking and a roadmap that names what must change before anything is built on it.
  • Secure landing zone and foundation automation: Policy evaluated before a change reaches a project rather than reported after an audit finds it, with native controls implemented rather than bolted alongside.
  • Zero trust access: Access decided by identity and device posture rather than by network position, which is the control that survives an estate spread across cloud, office and home.
  • Managed detection and response: Monitoring, threat hunting and incident response against a correlated view of the estate, with data sources from on premises and cloud landing in one place.
  • Compliance as code: Regulatory requirements expressed as policy the pipeline evaluates, so evidence is produced as work happens rather than assembled by hand the week an auditor asks.

Cloud migration

The move itself, where each workload takes the route it warrants and every cutover has a rehearsed way back.

  • Assessing cloud preparedness: Establish the target state per workload and the business case behind it, so the migration plan is an output of the assessment rather than an assumption it was built on.
  • Migration routes: Rehost, replatform, refactor, replace or re-engineer, decided per workload against what each will cost to run afterward rather than what it costs to move today.
  • Factory based execution: Repeatable waves with the same tooling, checks and evidence each time, because a migration improvised per workload is one that cannot be scheduled or staffed honestly.
  • Data and image migration: Continuous replication while the source stays live, so the cutover is a short decision that can be verified and postponed rather than a weekend nobody can reverse.
  • Cutover and rollback: Every wave rehearsed alongside the way back, including the dependencies that only reveal themselves when something is switched off.

Frequently Asked Questions

We chose Google Cloud for BigQuery. Where does the rest of the estate go?

Wherever serves the reason you chose it, which usually means less movement than a migration program assumes and more attention to who can reach the thing you bought.

When a specific capability drives the decision, the failure we see is not a bad migration. It is a good migration of everything, after which the capability is still only used by the team that asked for it, because the landing zone made reaching it a project each time.

So the foundation gets designed around access to that capability first. Which projects can query it, under what governance, with cost attributed to whoever runs the query. Then workloads move in the order that increases who can use it, rather than in the order that is easiest to schedule.

Some of the estate should not move at all, and saying so early is worth more than a wave plan that quietly assumes everything does.

Sources: Google Cloud Architecture Framework

Lift and shift, or modernize first?

Per workload, decided on what it costs to run afterward rather than what it costs to move. A program that picks one answer for the whole estate has replaced evidence with preference.

Lift and shift is right more often than modernization vendors suggest. If an application is stable, peripheral and nobody intends to change it, refactoring spends money on code that will never be touched again. Move it, right size it, leave it alone.

Modernization earns its place where the application is genuinely central, will be invested in for years, and has a shape that cloud economics punish. A monolith that scales only vertically is the clearest case, because in cloud you pay for that shape monthly.

Most estates end up mixed. What matters is that the split was decided per application during assessment rather than inherited from whichever approach the program was sold on.

Sources: Google Cloud migration path

What does GKE Enterprise actually buy us over plain GKE?

Fleet level management, policy and service mesh across many clusters. If you run a handful of clusters in one organization, standard GKE is very likely enough.

The case for the enterprise tier is scale and heterogeneity: dozens of clusters, several teams who each want autonomy, workloads that have to run outside Google Cloud as well, and a need to apply configuration and policy once rather than per cluster. That is a real problem and it is expensive to solve by hand.

It is not a substitute for deciding whether you need Kubernetes at all. A service that fits Cloud Run does not become better by joining a fleet, and clusters that exist because a platform team wanted them will cost more than the workloads justify.

We would size this after the workload assessment rather than before it. The cluster count that a real estate needs is usually smaller than the one it currently has.

Sources: GKE Enterprise documentation

How do you migrate databases without a long outage?

Continuous replication, then a short cutover once the replica has caught up and been verified. The outage is minutes rather than a weekend for most engines.

The managed migration service handles the common paths with change data capture running while the source stays live. The cutover is then a decision rather than an event, which also means it can be rehearsed and, if the verification fails, postponed without drama.

What takes the time is not the replication. It is the applications, which usually carry connection strings, driver assumptions and occasionally a stored procedure nobody can explain. Heterogeneous moves between engines are a different scale of problem again.

So we would want the application dependency work done before the database is touched. A database that cuts over cleanly into applications nobody tested has moved the outage rather than avoided it.

Sources: Database Migration Service

Is Gemini production ready for our use case?

That depends entirely on the use case and on whether you are prepared to measure it, which is the part that gets skipped.

For retrieval over your own documents, summarization, extraction and classification, the models are genuinely capable and the platform work around them is the real project: grounding in your corpus, evaluation, guardrails, and deciding what the system does when it should refuse rather than answer.

Where programs go wrong is treating a demonstration as evidence. A model that performs well on ten hand picked examples tells you very little about the long tail, and the long tail is where the reputational risk lives.

So we would build the evaluation set before choosing the model, from your own material, including the cases you expect to be hard. That takes a couple of weeks and it changes the answer often enough that we treat it as standard rather than optional.

Sources: Vertex AI documentation

What does BeyondCorp change day to day?

Access stops depending on where a request came from and starts depending on who is asking and what they are asking from. In practice that means the VPN stops being the thing standing between people and their work.

The security argument is that network position was never a good proxy for trust, and in an estate spread across cloud, office and home it has stopped being any proxy at all. The practical argument is usually more persuasive to the people affected: access that works the same everywhere.

The work is in the policy, not the product. Which applications, which device postures, which contexts, and what happens to the exceptions. Every organization has systems that cannot participate yet, and pretending otherwise is how these programs stall.

We would phase it, starting with applications where the policy is uncontroversial, and keep the legacy path until the exceptions have somewhere to go.

Sources: BeyondCorp Enterprise

Should we move to Google Workspace?

It is a change management project with a technical component, not the reverse, and programs that get that ratio wrong are the ones that struggle.

The migration itself is well trodden. Mail, files and calendar move with history and permissions, and the tooling is mature. What decides success is identity, retention and habit: single sign on agreed up front, regulatory holds carried across rather than quietly dropped, and enough support that people do not quietly keep working in the old place.

The honest caution is integration. Most organizations have a line of business system that assumes a particular mail platform, and finding those late is what turns a clean migration into a long tail of exceptions.

We would inventory those first. If the list is short the move is straightforward. If it is long, that is worth knowing before a date is announced.

Sources: Google Workspace admin documentation

How do you avoid a different lock in on Google Cloud?

By being deliberate about where you accept it, because portability is not free and pretending to avoid lock in usually costs more than accepting it consciously.

Containers, open table formats and standard interfaces genuinely travel, and where they fit they are worth choosing. Managed services are stickier by design, and that stickiness is frequently what you are paying for. A warehouse you could move in a weekend is not the warehouse you wanted.

The question worth asking is what would actually trigger a move, and what it would cost if it happened. If nobody can answer that, the portability being defended is theoretical, and the architecture compromises made to preserve it are real.

We would rather document the exit path for the few things that genuinely need one than build the whole estate to a lowest common denominator nobody exercises.

Sources: Google Cloud Architecture Framework

How is security handled during the migration itself?

The foundation is built secure before workloads arrive, because retrofitting boundaries onto an estate that already depends on them is a considerably larger project.

Organization structure, identity, network segmentation and policy come first. That is unglamorous and it is the cheapest security work available: a boundary set before anything depends on it is configuration, and the same boundary set afterward is a migration with a compliance deadline attached.

During the move itself, the risk that actually materializes is usually mundane. Production data copied into a project for testing, a permission widened to unblock a wave and never narrowed, a service account key created for convenience. Those need detection rather than good intentions.

So posture monitoring runs from the first project rather than from go live. Finding a misconfiguration during migration is a correction. Finding it afterward is an incident.

Sources: Google Cloud security foundations

How do you keep Google Cloud spend from drifting?

By attributing it to the team that can change it, which requires project and folder structure to be decided in the foundation rather than reconstructed later.

The characteristic drift on Google Cloud is query and inference cost: a warehouse where a scheduled report scans far more than it needs, or a model endpoint left running between experiments. Neither shows up as an obvious line item until the bill consolidates them.

So cost controls belong in the platform. Query limits and slot commitments sized against measured use, lifecycle policies on storage, and shared infrastructure apportioned rather than pooled into a number no single team owns.

The measure that matters is not the total. It is whether the person who could reduce a line can see that line without asking anyone.

Sources: FinOps Foundation framework

Can you work with our existing platform team?

Usually, and it is the shape that works best. Your team holds the estate history, which is the part that cannot be usefully outsourced.

The common split is that we bring the platform engineering and the migration disciplines that are hard to staff for a single program, while your team keeps the relationships with application owners and the judgment about what actually matters. We would expect to hand over rather than to stay.

What matters more than the split is who owns the decisions. Programs go wrong when ownership is ambiguous, not when the team is mixed, so we would want that written down at the start including which calls are escalations.

If we are still essential a year after go live, the platform was designed wrong.

Sources: Google Cloud Architecture Framework

What is the smallest sensible engagement?

A landing zone and migration read. A few weeks, a defined output, and no obligation to do anything afterward.

It covers the current estate and its dependencies, which workloads justify which migration route, what the organization and identity structure should be, where the regulated data sits, and which applications should be retired rather than moved. For an existing Google Cloud footprint it also covers where the running state has drifted from whatever was designed.

The output is a written position and a sequence, structured so that leave it alone is an available conclusion for any individual workload. Some estates are in better shape than their owners assume.

Everything larger is a decision you can make afterward with better information than you have now.
