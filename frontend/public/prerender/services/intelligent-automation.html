Frequently Asked Questions

How do we know which processes are actually worth automating?

Volume, exception rate and the cost of handling a case wrong. A process run ten thousand times a month with a low exception rate is the obvious candidate; one run twice a week with constant judgment calls almost never is, however painful it feels to the team doing it.

The number that decides it is not headcount saved, it is straight-through rate — the share of cases that complete without a person touching them. A process that automates to sixty per cent straight-through and dumps the rest into a queue has moved the work rather than removed it.

Process mining answers this properly because it shows how the process actually runs rather than how the process document says it runs. That gap is where most failed automations live, and two weeks of mining is a cheaper way to find it than six months of building.

Sources: Kangqore Business Process Management

Our last RPA program stalled. Why would this one be different?

Most stall in the same place: bots got built, nothing coordinated them, and within a year the estate was a set of scripts with no owner. The build was never the hard part. What was missing was the orchestration layer and somebody accountable for the process rather than the robot.

The second common cause is starting with the tool. A platform gets bought, a Center of Excellence gets stood up around it, and the CoE becomes a request queue instead of a capability. The fifth automation then costs the same as the first, which is the clearest sign that nothing is being reused.

We would want to see what you already have before promising anything. Often the answer is that the existing estate is salvageable and needs orchestration and ownership rather than replacement.

What happens when the underlying application changes?

Something breaks — that is the honest answer, and any vendor who says otherwise is selling. What differs is whether you find out from monitoring or from a customer complaint three weeks later.

We reduce the surface where possible: an API is used in preference to screen automation every time the target system allows it, because an API contract changes on a release note and a user interface changes whenever somebody moves a button. Where screen automation is unavoidable, selectors are built to be as resilient as the platform allows and the failure path is monitored explicitly.

Then it is an ownership question. Every automation has a named owner and a runbook, so when a change lands there is a person whose job it is to fix it rather than a ticket nobody recognizes.

Do you build on our platform licenses, or bring your own?

Yours, wherever you already have them. If you own UiPath or Automation Anywhere seats, or Power Automate is included in your Microsoft agreement, that usually decides the platform regardless of what we might otherwise prefer.

Where there is no incumbent, the choice is driven by your estate rather than by benchmarks. Microsoft-heavy organizations get a different recommendation from ones running mainframe green screens, and the presence of unstructured documents pulls the decision toward whichever intelligent document processing engine handles your formats best.

Under Automation-as-a-Service we hold the licensing and you consume the capability, which is the one case where the platform is genuinely ours rather than yours.

How many processes do we need before this pays for itself?

Fewer than most business cases assume, but the first one rarely does it alone. The economics turn on reuse: the first automation carries the cost of standing up the pipeline, the standards and the monitoring, and everything after it should be materially cheaper.

If your fifth automation costs what your first one did, the program is not compounding and something is wrong with how components are being built rather than with the business case.

This is why the proof-of-value engagement is scoped as one process taken far enough to measure. It gives you a real number from your own estate rather than an industry average that was never about your processes.

Who runs the bots after go-live?

Whoever you decide, and it should be decided before the build rather than after. The three workable models are your team with our support, a joint operation during a handover period, or fully managed by us under an agreed service level.

What does not work is leaving it implicit. Automations that go live without a named operational owner are the ones that fail quietly, because nobody is watching the dashboard and nobody has been told they should be.

If you take it in-house we hand over the repository, the runbooks, the monitoring configuration and the documented failure modes. Nothing needs us present to keep running.

How is this different from your BPM and RPA services?

RPA is the digital worker doing the clicking. BPM is the process design and the workflow layer. Intelligent Automation is the combination, with AI added where the work needs judgment or reads unstructured content.

In practice the boundary matters less than the sequence. Almost nobody should buy RPA first: without process work you automate a bad process faster, and without orchestration you get bots that cannot be sequenced.

If you are certain the requirement is narrow — one rule-based process, well documented, no unstructured input — the RPA service is a cheaper entry point and we will say so.

Sources: Robotic Process Automation · Business Process Management

Can you automate processes that need human judgment?

Partly, and the useful framing is which part rather than whether. Most judgment-heavy processes are eighty per cent mechanical work wrapped around a decision that genuinely needs a person. Automating the mechanical part and routing the decision is usually worth more than attempting the whole thing.

Where a model supports the decision, a confidence threshold decides what it handles and what escalates. Set deliberately, that threshold is the control that keeps automation safe; left at a vendor default, it is the reason a system quietly makes decisions nobody sanctioned.

For decisions affecting individuals — credit, claims, eligibility — the oversight requirement is not optional. Under the EU AI Act several of these are classified high-risk, which brings documentation and human oversight duties that are cheaper to design in than to retrofit.

Sources: EU AI Act · Kangqore AI Governance

How do you handle documents that are not standard forms?

Intelligent document processing handles far more variation than templated extraction did, but it is not magic and the honest ceiling depends on your documents rather than on the engine.

Clean, structured documents are close to a solved problem and should be bought rather than built. Handwritten annotations, poor scans, documents where the meaning depends on a clause elsewhere, and formats that vary by supplier are where accuracy falls and where a validation step earns its place.

We would want to run a sample of your worst documents rather than your best ones before quoting anything. The best ones tell you nothing you did not already assume.

Sources: Kangqore AI & Cognitive Computing

What happens to the people whose work gets automated?

That is your decision rather than ours, but it determines whether the program succeeds, so it is worth settling early. Automation programs that are positioned as headcount reduction meet exactly the resistance you would expect from the people whose process knowledge the build depends on.

The programs that land well move people onto the exception work and the judgment calls, which is the part of the job that was always the skilled part and was being crowded out by volume.

Practically, the people who run a process today are the best source of truth about how it actually works, including the workarounds that never made it into the documentation. Involving them early is both the decent thing and the thing that makes the automation correct.
